A Book in Seven Volumes

Seven Lanterns

Seven books from the Harvard class everyone suddenly wants to take: wrath, wandering, exile, love in fragments, a locked door, a sacred text read twice, and a veil.

The Prologue

Gentle reader, draw near. Upon this shelf there stand seven ancient souls, each a lantern lit against the dark. Here wrath is sung, and wandering, and exile. Here love is broken into fragments, and justice locked behind a door. Here holy words are read anew, and a people speaketh from behind a veil. Turn thou the page, and I shall tell their tales.

Book IHOMER
I

The Iliad

HOMER · c. 8th century BCE · Epic in 24 books

Key Extracts

  • Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans.

    Book I · Samuel Butler translation, 1898

  • Men come and go as leaves year by year upon the trees.

    Book VI · Samuel Butler translation, 1898

  • Old King Priam kneels before Achilles and kisses the hands that killed his son. Achilles weeps for his own father, and the two enemies grieve together.

    Book XXIV · In essence

The Bard’s Telling

Sing, Muse, of wrath, of great Achilles’ wrath, that bitter root from whence a thousand sorrows grew. For one slight to his honour he forsook the field, and Greeks did fall like wheat before the scythe. Yet mark the ending, gentle friend. Not in triumph doth the story close, but in a tent at midnight, where an old king kneels, and kisses the hands that slew his son. And the slayer weeps, remembering his own father. So learn thou this: that rage doth make us monsters, and grief, when shared, doth make us men again.

Home is not a place you return to by force. It is earned with wit, patience and the choice of a mortal life over an easy forever.

Book IIHOMER
II

The Odyssey

HOMER · c. 8th century BCE · Epic in 24 books

Key Extracts

  • Tell me, O muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy.

    Book I · Samuel Butler translation, 1900

  • My name is Noman; this is what my father and mother and my friends have always called me.

    Book IX · Odysseus tricks the Cyclops · Butler, 1900

  • There is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house.

    Book VI · Butler, 1900

The Bard’s Telling

Now tell, O Muse, of him, the man of many turns, who twenty years did wander o’er the wine-dark sea. Not by strength alone came he home, but by his wit. To the one-eyed giant he named himself No One, and so escaped. Fair nymphs did offer him to live forever, yet he chose a mortal wife, a rocky isle, a hearth grown cold. What is home, then, but the thing we choose above eternity? And she, his patient queen, did weave by day and unweave by night, as cunning as her lord. Two minds, one house. There lies the voyage’s end.

Take with you

Aeneas carries his father out of the fire and leads his son by the hand: the past and the future, held at once. Leadership is often that weight.

Book IIIVIRGIL
III

The Aeneid

VIRGIL · 29–19 BCE · Epic in 12 books

Key Extracts

  • Arms, and the man I sing, who, forc’d by fate, / And haughty Juno’s unrelenting hate, / Expell’d and exil’d, left the Trojan shore.

    Book I · John Dryden translation, 1697

  • Forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit.

    Book I, line 203 · “Perhaps one day it will please us to remember even these things.”

  • Sunt lacrimae rerum et mentem mortalia tangunt.

    Book I, line 462 · “There are tears in things, and what is mortal touches the mind.”

The Bard’s Telling

Of arms I sing, and of the man whom fate did drive from burning Troy. Upon his shoulders bore he his aged father, and by the hand he led his little son. And though Queen Dido loved him, duty called him hence, and he did go, and she did die. Alas, that greatness costs so dear! Yet in the darkest storm he spake unto his weary men: perchance, one day, ’twill please us to remember even this. So build we cities from our ruins, and bear our past upon our backs, toward a future we shall never see.

Book IVSAPPHO
IV

If Not, Winter

SAPPHO · c. 630–570 BCE · Lyric fragments

Key Extracts

  • Some say a host of horsemen, some say foot soldiers, some say ships are the most beautiful thing on the dark earth. I say it is whatever one loves.

    Fragment 16 · Rendered from the Greek for this book

  • Eros, the loosener of limbs, shakes me again: bittersweet, unmanageable, a creature that creeps in.

    Fragment 130 · Rendered from the Greek

  • Someone, I tell you, will remember us, even in another time.

    Fragment 147 · Rendered from the Greek

The Bard’s Telling

Of all her nine books, sweet Sappho, tenth of the Muses, little now remains: some scraps of papyrus, torn, and wrapped about the dead. Yet hear how much a fragment holds! Some say the fairest thing upon the dark earth is a host of horse, or ships in line. But she saith, it is whatever one doth love. Love, that limb-loosener, bitter-sweet, creeps on us like a serpent. And mark what the silence speaks: where words are lost, the heart doth fill the gap. Someone, she promised, will remember us. Lo, we do.

Book VFRANZ KAFKA
V

The Trial

FRANZ KAFKA · Written 1914–15 · Published 1925

Key Extracts

  • Someone must have slandered Josef K., for without having done anything wrong he was arrested one morning.

    Opening line · Translated from the German original, 1925

  • Before the Law stands a doorkeeper.

    Chapter 9, ‘In the Cathedral’ · The parable begins

  • No one else could be admitted here, since this entrance was meant only for you. I am now going to shut it.

    The doorkeeper’s last words to the dying man

The Bard’s Telling

Someone must have slandered Josef K., for though he did no wrong, one morning he was taken. And never learnt he his offence, nor saw the judge, nor found the court that tried him. Hear now this parable. Before the Law there stands a keeper of the gate. A countryman did wait there all his life, and, dying, asked why none but he had come. Quoth the keeper: this door was made for thee alone, and now I shut it. O reader, how oft we wait for leave that no one is withholding! Knock, and question, and walk through.

Book VIJAMES L. KUGEL
VI

How to Read the Bible

JAMES L. KUGEL · 2007 · A guide to Scripture, then and now

Key Extracts

  • Ancient interpreters made four assumptions: Scripture is cryptic, it speaks directly to every reader, it is perfect and harmonious, and every word is divinely given.

    Key idea · Summarised

  • Modern scholarship reads the same chapters as a human, historical work, assembled by many hands over centuries.

    Key idea · Summarised

  • Kugel sets the two readings side by side, chapter by chapter, and asks what it means to read Scripture once you have seen both.

    The book’s method · Summarised

The Bard’s Telling

How shall we read the oldest book of all? Two ways there be, saith our learned guide. The ancients read it as a riddle, full of hidden meaning, each verse a lesson for this very day, without a flaw, and every word from heaven. The moderns read it as a work of men: of scribes and kings and many hands, stitched across the centuries. Betwixt these two, a chasm yawns. Yet both do teach us this: that reading is itself an act of faith or doubt, and how we read doth shape the thing we find.

Book VIIW. E. B. DU BOIS
VII

The Souls of Black Folk

W. E. B. DU BOIS · 1903 · Essays

Key Extracts

  • The problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line.

    The Forethought · 1903

  • This sense of always looking at one’s self through the eyes of others.

    Chapter I, ‘Of Our Spiritual Strivings’ · On double-consciousness

  • I sit with Shakespeare and he winces not.

    Chapter VI, ‘Of the Training of Black Men’

The Bard’s Telling

The problem of this age, quoth he, is the colour-line. Behind a veil was he born, and from behind it saw the world: a strange and twofold sense, ever to look upon one’s self through the eyes of others. Two souls, two strivings, warring in one breast. Yet hear his triumph! I sit with Shakespeare, saith he, and he winces not. Into the realm of thought there is no veil, no gate, no keeper. Mind meeteth mind, and is made free. O let the sorrow songs be sung, for in them sleeps a people’s soul.

Epilogue

Close the cover

Thus endeth our small volume. Seven books, and one lesson writ in each: that to be human is to rage and grieve, to wander and come home, to love in fragments, to knock at doors, to read, and to be read. Close now the cover, gentle friend. The shelf will keep them till thou com’st again.
